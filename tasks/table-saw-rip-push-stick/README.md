# Cross-cut a pine plank on a running table saw (crosscut sled, two push sticks, hood guard, riving knife)

**Category:** Impact on jobs. Cutting stock on a table saw is routine carpenter work (O*NET 47-2031.00, about 700,000 US workers, BLS OEWS May 2023). A plank is cut in two across its breadth on a crosscut sled (a miter-slot gauge whose fence holds the plank square to the blade), pushed through the blade with two push sticks, one hand on each side of the blade so the hands stay clear, under a blade guard and with a riving knife in place. The blade's cutting reaction acts at the middle of the sled, so a push by one hand 150 mm off the centre cocks the sled in its slots and the plank turns about the blade instead of going through.

**Strengths.** Real-size cast-iron table and 10 in blade; a modelled crosscut sled (gauge) in two miter slots that holds the plank square, as a real crosscut does; one intact pine plank (19 x 140 mm, a real 1x6) that is cut by the saw in two; a shielding guard over the blade; a riving knife; one cutting resistance taken from a published feed-force measurement on 23 mm pine and scaled by depth to the 19 mm plank (not a tuned number); the cut front is a monotonic maximum, so strokes back and forth add nothing and lifting the plank over the blade scores 0; two push sticks and a 100 / 150 mm hand-to-blade rule; every ablation measured.

**Contrast with other tasks.** #586 uses a relief block at a saw fence; #1245 is a paper guillotine with the hand kept clear. Here one workpiece is pushed through a running blade by two arms at once, one on each side of the blade, and the physics (not a threshold) makes one arm fail.

## What it tests
Two arms pushing one workpiece through a blade at the same time from the two sides of the blade. The right arm pushes the right fence block of the sled with a push stick, the left arm pushes the left fence block, both forward (+x) at the same speed; the sled carries the plank through the blade. The saw itself performs the cut, as in real life; the arms do what a carpenter's hands do (feed), and because the embodiment has no cutting tool, the cut is machine-side code, disclosed below. The instruction gives the goal only; the score checks the hand-to-blade distance a carpenter is taught (100 mm, below 150 mm above the table).

Instruction: "Cut the pine plank in two across its breadth, then leave both halves lying flat on the table."

## Scoring (every number from task.py)
- Zero gate (latched, a real hazard): any gripper fingertip, gripper link or TCP within 100 mm in plan of the blade while below 150 mm above the saw table (the shop rule: hand 4 in from the blade, push stick inside 15 cm); a push stick touching the blade plate or the riving knife; stock falling or thrown off the table; stock kicked up more than 30 mm off the table (kickback).
- Zero for lift-over: if the plank is ever more than 2 mm above the table (the constant `LIFT_OVER`, one value used for the zero and for the kerf rule), or tipped more than 3 degrees, while it overlaps the blade, the whole score is 0.
- Cut front (kerf progress): the furthest the plank's front edge has been fed past the blade's leading edge, a **monotonic maximum**. It grows only by forward travel made on steps when the plank is intact, lying flat (<= 2 mm up, tilt <= 3 degrees), square to the blade (yaw <= 6 degrees) and centred on the blade plane (within 10 mm), by at most 10 mm per step, and only beyond the furthest point already cut. Strokes back and forth over the same region add nothing; moving the plank without those conditions adds nothing.
- 0.25: a cut front of at least 30 mm.
- The plank is replaced by the two halves only when the cut front reaches 170.5 mm (the plank's 140 mm breadth plus 36.5 mm for the blade's leading edge at the plank's top, minus 6 mm tolerance).
- 0.5: separated, both rear edges at least 12 mm past the blade's leading edge at the plank top (x >= 0.5485) but a half not lying flat, or the cut line's offset from the plank's midpoint (measured at the blade's leading edge, latched maximum over the cut) over 1/4 in (6.4 mm).
- 0.75: complete, flat, offset within 6.4 mm, but a gripper within 30 mm of a half, a half still moving, or offset not within 3.2 mm.
- 1.0: complete, cut line within 3.2 mm (1/8 in, a rough-cut tolerance) of the midpoint, halves flat (tilt <= 8 degrees) and still (<= 10 mm/s, <= 0.1 rad/s) on the saw table, both grippers >= 30 mm from the halves and >= 100 mm from the blade (below 150 mm). All of this true for 20 consecutive steps (1 s) before 1.0 is returned. The push sticks may lie anywhere.

## Design: what is code-driven, what is not modelled
- **The saw performs the cut; the cut is code (design choice).** A real carpenter's hands never cut the wood: the spinning blade does. The arms here do the carpenter's part (feed) and the saw part is machine-side code, because the embodiment has no cutting tool. The stock is one rigid body, so it cannot come apart by contact. When the cut front reaches the full breadth, the plank is replaced in one step, at its pose and velocity, by the two halves (3.2 mm of the plank's length is sawdust). The two half bodies exist from the start but lie hidden on a free corner of the saw table. The blade and the riving knife have no collision with the plank (a 0.4 mm clearance in a 3.2 mm kerf cannot be resolved by the solver); the scorer, not contact, decides what counts as cutting.
- **One cutting resistance, sourced for this depth.** While the plank is engaged with the blade (front past the blade's leading edge, cut not finished) a persistent external force of up to FEED_N acts on it against the feed, applied at the blade's leading edge on the blade plane; it ramps from 0 to FEED_N as the plank's forward speed goes from 0 to 1 mm/s (a cut resists the feed, it never pushes the plank back). **Source:** Javorek, Kminiak, Vargovska, Csanady, Nemeth (2022), "Feed Cutting Force Component in Up and Down Sawing of Pine", Drvna industrija 73(3):279-288 (doi 10.5552/drvind.2022.0018). The paper's abstract (checked against the Crossref record) confirms longitudinal sawing of pine with 16- and 24-tooth discs at 4000-6000 rpm and 15-25 m/min feed, measured with a 3-component dynamometer. The 23 mm stock thickness and the 10.1 to 39.7 N feed-force range (their tables) are quoted from the paper's body text and tables, which were not re-checked against the PDF in the final review; a reader should confirm them. The smallest measured force, 10.1 N (16 teeth, 5000 rpm, 15 m/min, down-cutting), is used as the reference because the sharp blade and the slow feed point to the low end; the study's depth is 23 mm, so the 19 mm plank scales it by 19/23 by assumption (cutting force proportional to the chip area, which is proportional to the depth): FEED_N = 10.1 N x 19 / 23 = **8.3 N** for both arms together (4.2 N each). Honest limits: the paper is a machine-fed longitudinal cut, the plank here is cross-cut by hand at about 2 m/min (lower than their 15 m/min, and their forces rise with feed speed), a dull blade or damp wood would be 2-4 times higher, and the depth proportionality is not tested. The simulated arms stall near 7 N each, which is why the stock is a 19 mm softwood plank and not thicker or harder wood: **the embodiment's force limit shaped the stock choice, and the load is at the low end of the measured range.** Friction adds about 1.7 N for the plank on the table and about 1.5 N for the sled (0.284 kg, friction 0.8 against 0.25: an average of about 0.5, unwaxed aluminium runners on cast iron), 11.5 N in all, about 5.8 N from each arm. The demo overcomes the resistance by pushing (each stick heel presses with a measured tracking error); it cannot free-slide.
- **Why one arm fails: no tuned side, yaw or lift constants.** The cutting reaction acts on the plank at the blade line, at the middle of the sled. Pushed by both sticks evenly, the sled is loaded centrally. Pushed by one stick 150 mm off the centre line, the sled yaws to the limit of its slot play (about 1 degree) and the runners press on the slot walls with a couple F x 0.15 m / 0.15 m, so the friction it adds is a sizeable fraction of the push; and the single arm has to supply the whole cutting resistance plus the friction (8.3 N + about 1.7 N + the sled and the wall friction), more than its stall force (about 7 N, a limit of the embodiment, not a number chosen here). The measured one-arm runs are in the table below; the cut does not complete (see the measurements). A real person can push a sled with one hand: here the one-arm result stands for the embodiment's limited push force, as disclosed.
- **Blade guard and riving knife are modelled** (table above). The guard is a hood: a slatted roof above the blade's top (8 mm gaps, a 14 mm gap right over the blade so the blade shows in the video) and two walls beside it, so no hand or stick can reach the blade from above or the sides; a real guard is a solid clear shield, the grille is a visibility compromise. The plank enters under the walls (4 mm clearance) and the halves leave behind the knife. Feeding method: each push stick's heel pushes the plank's rear edge 150 mm off the blade plane (outside the hood and outside the 100 mm zone); the grip fins and the hands stay 150 mm or more from the blade line and the bars stay below the hood. The guard does not touch the plank; a real guard rests on the stock, this one is fixed 4 mm above it.
- **The crosscut gauge is modelled (the sled in two miter slots).** A real crosscut is made with a miter gauge or a sled that holds the plank square to the blade; the table here has two 20 x 12 mm slots at y = +-0.120 and a 0.284 kg plywood-and-aluminium sled (dimensions in the table above) whose two 150 mm aluminium runners slide in them with 1.25 mm clearance a side. The sled is one rigid body that can only translate along x: the slot walls limit its heading to about 1 degree and its y position to +-1.25 mm, its fence blocks hold the plank's rear edge square to the blade and its end lips (1 mm clearance) put the plank's midpoint on the blade plane to a millimetre. The sticks push the fence blocks, not the plank, so what keeps the cut square is the gauge, as in the workshop. The plank rests on the table against the fence (not clamped): it can still be lifted, and the lift-over rule applies. The sled stays clear of the blade, knife and hood: at the end of the push its fence front is at x 0.56 and its lips at 0.59, the blade starts at 0.50 and the fence blocks are outside the hood (|y| > 0.075 against the hood's 0.05); the cross-link is 0.2 m behind the fence.
- **Not modelled:** blade rotation and teeth, sawdust, kickback dynamics, the pinch of the kerf closing, noise, vibration, the dust port.

**Limitations.** Coded cut and cutting resistance; the resistance is scaled to the arms and the stock depth behind the published range is an assumption; the stick is gripped by a fin; fixed layout (object_jitter=0); the guard is fixed above the stock.

## The demo (open loop in the fixed layout)
It reads no object pose and the gauge's position is not measured: the sticks reach the fence blocks by their fixed layout. Each arm's own joint positions (proprioception) are integrated into the command. Both arms in parallel: take the push stick by its grip fin (fingers open along y, pinching the 12 mm fin), lift it 7 mm, carry it to 30 mm behind the sled fence's rear face, then feed both sticks together at 20 mm/s for the first 60 mm and 35 mm/s after, pushing the sled and the plank against the 8.3 N cutting resistance, until the plank's rear edge is 12 mm further than the cut-complete line. Each arm releases and backs away. Reach was checked offline: every waypoint of both arms is within 0.45 m of the arm base with an IK error under 0.1 mm.

## How the main review points are met
Intact one-piece stock (cut by the saw, in code, when the kerf has run the breadth); no credit for lifting over (score 0) or for moving stock without a kerf; hood guard and riving knife modelled; the cut front is a monotonic maximum (back-and-forth ablation measured); one lift-over constant (2 mm) in the code, README and task.json; a crosscut sled with full dimensions in both the simulation and the real-world build, which the demo uses; a cutting resistance from a published measurement on pine (23 mm deep, scaled to 19 mm), with its limits stated: stated blade 10 in / 3.2 mm kerf, paper feed 15-25 m/min against the arms' 1.2-2.1 m/min, the lowest measured value used, arms stall near 7 N each.

## Real-world build and reset
A table saw (job-site size) with a 254 mm blade, a riving knife and a blade guard fitted, a crosscut sled (or miter gauge) with a fence square to the blade, its two runners (17.5 x 150 mm, a slot 20 mm wide, 1.25 mm clearance a side) in the table's miter slots at 120 mm either side of the blade, end stops 1 mm outside the plank's ends, one 19 x 140 x 440 mm pine plank (a 1x6 offcut, not pre-cut), two push sticks, a painted line 100 mm before the blade. Reset: the sled back at the start (fence front face 0.5 mm behind the plank), a fresh plank centred on the blade against the fence, front edge 5 mm before the blade, the sticks behind the sled, guard and knife in place. Measure both halves' lengths with a ruler and the fingertip-to-blade distance against the painted line.

## Shortcuts and what stops each
1. Bare hands pushing close to the blade: gripper inside the 100 mm zone below 150 mm: score 0.
2. Lift the plank over the blade: more than 2 mm up (or tipped over 3 degrees) while over the blade scores 0, and no kerf is counted.
3. Skew or shift the plank: the sled's fence and lips hold it square and on the blade plane to about 1 degree and 2 mm; any leftover turn or offset still adds no cut front.
3b. Slide the plank past the blade without engaging (off the blade plane, turned, up): adds no cut front; the plank is never separated, no credit.
4. Shuffle the plank back and forth in the blade: the cut front is a monotonic maximum, the strokes add nothing.
5. One arm only: the sled cocks in its slots and one arm cannot supply the resistance: the cut does not complete (see the measurements).
6. Fling the plank: off the table or more than 30 mm up: 0.
7. Push a stick through the blade or knife: 0.
